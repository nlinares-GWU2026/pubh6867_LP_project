Creating the first Q2 visualization: After inspecting and the `q2_table` and pasting it into Claude, all 3 historical groups appeared to sit between 1.4% and 2.0% for LP in the 4,000 - 5,000 time window (Steppe = 2/122 = 1.6%, Bell Beaker = 3/147 = 2.0%, Other = 7/494 = 1.4%). When I checked the A_count (allele count) in this bin, I discovered that 789 zeros, 12 twos, and 0 ones made up the allele breakdown. Because ancient DNA samples are often sequenced at low coverage (one random allele read per person = **pseudohaploid**), it will get recorded as 0 (no allele) or 2 (has derived allele). This means that if a number like 4 appears, it means that *2 total individuals* out of 122 carry the allele, NOT 4 copies across the 244 alleles. So because of this, the percentages remain the same, but the bar labels must change to say 2/122 insteadof 4/244 which would double count the evidence.
    - I pasted the final output of the `carriers` to have Claude confirm the allele counts before moving to the visualization. Claude's reponse was: The check confirms it: 789 zeros, 12 twos, and no 1s. In this bin every person is a single allele read, so "2 of 122 individuals" is the honest way to state the steppe count. The totals line up too: 2 + 3 + 7 = 12 carriers.
    - To determine if these tiny percentage differences (1.6% vs 2.0% vs 1.4%) were meaningful or just random chance, I ran Fisher's exact test (`cmp()` function). The p-values were as follows: Steppe vs Others p = 0.69, Bell Beaker vs Others p = 0.70, Steppe vs Bell Beaker = 1.00. None of these was significant. The p-value of 0.69 means that a difference this small *would happen by pure chance 69% of the time*. So there is no statistical evidence that Steppe-associated groups carried the LP allele at a higher rate than other Europeans did during this period.
